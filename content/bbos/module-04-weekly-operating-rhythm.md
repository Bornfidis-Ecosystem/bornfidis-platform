*Bornfidis Business Operating System (BBOS) — Version 1 · Main Guide*

## Module purpose

In Modules 1–3 you built clarity, a sales system, and defensible prices. Those tools only help if they show up in a real week — while you are still cleaning, driving, quoting, and solving problems.

Most owners do not fail because they lack ideas. They fail because the week has no shape. Monday arrives, the phone rings, and the day is decided by whoever is loudest. Good intentions about follow-up, pricing, and planning wait for a quieter season that never comes.

Module 4 installs a **Weekly Operating Rhythm**: a repeatable way to run the business through one clear cycle:

```text
Plan → Deliver → Sell and Follow Up → Review → Close
```

This is not a perfect calendar. It is a small set of **blocks and anchors** that fit around customer work. You will design a rhythm you can keep, protect it when the week breaks, and recover without treating disruption as personal failure.

> **NOTE**
> BBOS is a practical operating system, not legal, tax, accounting, HR, medical, or financial advice. This module is about operating habits. It does not promise more revenue, fewer emergencies, or a specific income result.

> **DO THIS NOW**
> Put these in reach before you begin: your One-Page Business Snapshot (Module 1), your Pipeline and Follow-Up Cadence (Module 2), and your priced Offer Ladder plus Price-Hold Script (Module 3). Module 4 runs those tools weekly. It does not rebuild them.

**What you will complete in this module**

1. A written **Weekly Operating Rhythm** for your business.  
2. A scheduled **Weekly Planning Block** (25–40 minutes; target ~30).  
3. A scheduled **Pipeline and Follow-Up Block** (30–60 minutes; target ~45), including a light Money and Pricing Checkpoint.  
4. **2–3 Daily Anchors** (not a long daily task list).  
5. A short **delivery-quality routine**.  
6. One completed **Weekly Close**, including a brief **Next-Week Handoff**.  
7. A written **Catch-Up Protocol**.  
8. **4–6 Rhythm Protection Rules**.

**How to work through this module**
- Read once, then complete exercises in order.
- Choose days and windows that fit *your* delivery peaks. BBOS does not prescribe a universal Monday or a fixed clock time.
- Keep Daily Anchors to 2–3. More is not better.
- Remember: a basic rhythm can begin immediately. It gets stronger as your pipeline and pricing systems mature.

## Chapter 4.1 — The Week as the Unit of Control

Days are too noisy to steer by. Months are too long to correct in time. The **week** is the right unit of control for a small service business.

A week is long enough to include real delivery, real follow-up, and a real review. It is short enough that you can notice drift and fix it before a whole quarter disappears.

### What “out of control” usually looks like

If any of these feel familiar, you do not need more motivation. You need a rhythm:

- You end the week busy but unsure what actually moved.
- Follow-ups live in your head until they go cold.
- Prices get soft when you are tired.
- Planning only happens when something breaks.
- Sunday night fills with dread, not a plan.

None of that means you are undisciplined. It means the business is running on reaction. Reaction is a mode. It is not a system.

### The BBOS Week

BBOS uses one operating cycle:

```text
Plan → Deliver → Sell and Follow Up → Review → Close
```

- **Plan** — choose the week’s priorities before the week chooses you.  
- **Deliver** — do the customer work to a defined standard.  
- **Sell and Follow Up** — keep the pipeline visible and moving.  
- **Review** — a light check on money and pricing signals (notice only).  
- **Close** — end the week honestly and hand one clean page to next week.

You already met a shorter version of this in Module 1. Module 4 makes it concrete enough to schedule.

### Why this cycle beats a to-do app

A long task list can still be reactive. You check boxes while the important Selling and Closing work never gets a seat. The BBOS Week forces a sequence:

- You **Plan** before you drown.  
- You **Deliver** without pretending delivery is the only job.  
- You **Sell and Follow Up** on purpose.  
- You **Review** lightly so money surprises do not stack up unnoticed.  
- You **Close** so the next week inherits clarity instead of residue.

The cycle is small enough to repeat when you are tired. That is the point. Fancy systems die in week three. Short blocks survive.

> **TIP**
> You do not need every block perfect in Week 1 of using this module. Start with Planning + Close. Add the Pipeline and Follow-Up Block as soon as you can protect ~45 minutes mid-week.

> **EXAMPLE — Maya's Property Services**
> Before a rhythm, Maya’s weeks looked like this: turnovers all day, texts at night, follow-ups “when she remembered,” and pricing decided under pressure. She was not lazy. She had no weekly container for Selling and Closing. *(Illustrative example only.)*

## Chapter 4.2 — Design Your Weekly Operating Rhythm

A rhythm is not a fantasy schedule with every hour labeled. It is a small set of protected **blocks** and daily **anchors** placed around the work you already do.

### Framework: The Weekly Operating Rhythm

Your written rhythm answers five practical questions:

1. When is my **Weekly Planning Block**?  
2. When is my **Pipeline and Follow-Up Block**?  
3. What are my **2–3 Daily Anchors**?  
4. When is my **Weekly Close**?  
5. What rules protect these when the week gets loud?

Choose windows that fit your business. A host-turnover business may plan Sunday evening and close Friday afternoon. Another owner may plan Monday morning and close Saturday. BBOS cares that the blocks exist and stay short — not that everyone uses the same day.

### How to choose windows without a perfect calendar

Use three practical tests:

1. **Energy test:** Can I do this block when I am still clear enough to tell the truth? Close especially needs honesty — do not place it at your worst crash moment if you can help it.  
2. **Delivery test:** Does this window usually survive my normal job load? If Wednesday mornings are always overbooked, do not put Pipeline there.  
3. **Boundary test:** Can I defend this window from “quick questions” for 30–45 minutes? If not, put it where you can close a door, sit in the car after a job, or work after the worker’s shift ends.

Write the window as a day + rough time of day (morning / midday / late afternoon / evening). Exact clock times are optional. Consistency of *placement* matters more than matching someone else’s timetable.

### Framework: Weekly Planning Block

**Duration:** 25–40 minutes. **Target:** about 30 minutes.

Use this block near the start of your week. Keep it short on purpose. If Planning regularly needs an hour, you are doing project work, not weekly control.

**Planning Block agenda (keep it tight):**
1. Glance at your One-Page Business Snapshot (one minute).  
2. Check real capacity: jobs already booked, worker hours, personal limits.  
3. Choose **top 3 outcomes** for the week (not 12).  
4. Confirm the windows for Pipeline/Follow-Up and Close.  
5. Note one delivery risk (travel, supplies, staffing, tough job).

Then stop. Do not rebuild your offer. Do not redesign your prices. Do not empty your entire inbox. Planning is direction, not completion.

Set a timer. When it rings, write the top 3 and the three windows, then leave. Unfinished inbox items wait for Daily Anchors or the Sell block. If you regularly finish Planning in under 20 minutes, that is fine — keep the reserved window so a heavier week still has room.

**Planning produces three visible outputs:** top 3 outcomes, confirmed Sell and Close windows, and one named delivery risk. If you leave with those three, the block worked.

### Capacity before ambition

The fastest way to break a rhythm is to plan a week your body and calendar cannot hold. Before you write top 3 outcomes, answer honestly:

- How many delivery hours are already committed?  
- What help do I actually have (worker hours, family limits, my own energy)?  
- What personal rest is non-negotiable this week?

If capacity is tight, your top 3 should shrink. Protecting the Pipeline and Follow-Up Block for 45 minutes in a heavy delivery week is a better outcome than pretending you will also redesign your website, write three SOPs, and clear every message. Ambition belongs in Module 8’s longer plan. The week belongs to reality.

### Place the rhythm using your Time Map

Your Module 1 Owner’s Time Map showed where hours go: Doing, Selling, Running, Building. Most owners in disorder have almost no Building time and scattered Selling time. Your Weekly Operating Rhythm deliberately creates small, repeatable spaces for Selling (Pipeline/Follow-Up) and Building (Planning + Close). Delivery still fills most of the week — that is normal.

> **WARNING**
> Do not design a rhythm that only works on a quiet week. Design one that survives a normal busy week. If your plan requires four free mornings, it will die in month one.

> **EXAMPLE — Maya's Property Services**
> Maya writes her rhythm around turnover peaks:
> - **Planning Block:** Sunday evening, ~30 minutes.  
> - **Pipeline and Follow-Up Block:** Wednesday late morning, ~45 minutes (after early turnovers).  
> - **Weekly Close:** Friday afternoon, ~35 minutes.  
> She does not schedule hour-by-hour. She protects three windows and lets delivery fill the rest. *(Illustrative example only.)*

**Exercise 4.2 — Write and schedule your rhythm**

Complete this on one page:
1. Weekly Planning Block — day/window + duration (25–40 min).  
2. Pipeline and Follow-Up Block — day/window + duration (30–60 min).  
3. Weekly Close — day/window + duration (25–45 min).  
4. One sentence: **“These windows fit my delivery reality because ____.”**

That page is your written Weekly Operating Rhythm for Chapter 4.2.

## Chapter 4.3 — Daily Anchors and Delivery Quality

Blocks run the week. **Daily Anchors** keep ordinary days from drifting into pure reaction.

### Framework: Daily Anchors

Daily Anchors are 2–3 small actions you repeat most working days. They should take minutes, not hours.

Good anchors are:
- Visible (you can tick them).  
- Short.  
- Connected to control (pipeline, delivery readiness, or quality).  

**Cap: 2–3 anchors.** If you list seven, you have written a second full-time job.

Examples (choose your own):
- Confirm tomorrow’s jobs and access needs.  
- Open the pipeline once and note the next action on any stuck opportunity.  
- After the last job, run a 60-second quality tick.

Anchors are not your whole day. Delivery still dominates. Anchors keep Selling and quality from disappearing for five days straight.

Choose anchors that match your friction points. If access chaos ruins mornings, make confirmation Anchor 1. If follow-ups die for days, make a short pipeline glance Anchor 2. If jobs leave unfinished notes, make the quality tick Anchor 3. Do not copy someone else’s list. Cap remains **2–3**.

### What to do when you miss an anchor

You will miss anchors. That is not the end of the rhythm. The rule is simple:

- Miss one day → resume the next working day.  
- Miss two days → resume and write why in your Close (one line).  
- Do not “make up” anchors with a guilt list of ten items on Saturday.

Consistency is built by returning, not by punishment.

### Framework: Delivery and Quality-Control Block

“Block” here does not mean a separate four-hour meeting. For most service owners, delivery *is* the week. The framework is a **routine wrapped around delivery**:

1. **Before:** know the scope (which Offer Ladder rung / Included standard).  
2. **During:** deliver to that standard.  
3. **After:** a short quality check — anything to redo, note, or tell your worker?

This is not Module 6 (Systems and Delegation). You are not writing full SOPs yet. You are installing a habit of finishing work *on purpose*, not just leaving the site tired.

If you have a part-time worker, Daily Anchors and quality checks are also how you coordinate without constant mid-job texts. A two-minute handoff beats ten interrupted messages.

> **TIP**
> Put your quality check at a natural edge — after the last job, before you drive home, or when you send the “job complete” message. Attach habits to edges you already have.

> **EXAMPLE — Maya's Property Services**
> Maya chooses **three Daily Anchors**:
> 1. Confirm next-day turnovers and key/access notes with her part-time worker.  
> 2. One pipeline glance (even 3 minutes) on working days.  
> 3. After each turnover, a quality tick: guest-ready standard met? supplies note?  
>
> She does not add “build the business for an hour every morning.” That would be fantasy. *(Illustrative example only.)*

**Exercise 4.3 — Define anchors and a quality routine**

2. Daily Anchor 2: ________________  
3. Optional Daily Anchor 3: ________________  
4. Delivery-quality check (3–5 bullets max): ________________

## Chapter 4.4 — Pipeline, Follow-Up, and Pricing Checkpoint

Delivery keeps today’s promises. The **Pipeline and Follow-Up Block** keeps tomorrow’s work from depending on luck.

You already built the tools in Module 2: a visible pipeline (Lead → Qualified → Proposed → Won / Lost), Should I Say Yes?, and a Follow-Up Cadence. Module 3 gave you priced rungs, a Price-Hold Script, and Discount Guardrails. This chapter is about **running** those tools weekly — not redesigning them.

### Framework: Pipeline and Follow-Up Block

**Duration:** 30–60 minutes. **Target:** about 45 minutes.  
This same sitting includes the light Money and Pricing Checkpoint.

**Suggested agenda (~45 minutes):**
1. **Update the pipeline (10–15 min)** — every live opportunity has a stage and a next action.  
2. **Qualify new Leads (5–10 min)** — run Should I Say Yes? where needed; Not now stays in Lead with a revisit date.  
3. **Execute the Follow-Up Cadence (10–15 min)** — do the messages/actions that are due; move silent opportunities toward Lost when the cadence ends.  
4. **Proposal prep (5–10 min)** — if something is ready for Proposed, prepare a specific Offer Ladder rung at a **defined price** (Module 3). Use your Price-Hold Script when you send or say the number.  
5. **Money and Pricing Checkpoint (5–10 min)** — see below.

If you only have 30 minutes, do steps 1–3. Do not skip updates and follow-ups to polish a proposal forever.

### Make the pipeline tell the truth

A pipeline that flatters you is useless. During the Sell block, be blunt:

- If there is no next action, add one or move the opportunity to Lost.  
- If you have not touched an opportunity past your Follow-Up Cadence end point, close it to Lost cleanly.  
- If a Lead has been sitting without Should I Say Yes?, run the filter now — Yes, Not now (with revisit date), or No.

Remember Module 2: **Not now** stays in **Lead** with a revisit date. It is not Qualified and it is not Lost. Your Sell block is where those revisit dates get honored.

### Active opportunities only

This block is for **active leads and current opportunities**. Following up past customers for retention, referrals, or reactivation belongs to Module 7. Keep Module 4’s Sell block focused so you actually finish it.

### Framework: Money and Pricing Checkpoint

This is **Review** in the BBOS Week — and it stays light on purpose.

**Module 4 notices; Module 5 analyzes.**

In this checkpoint, only ask:

- Did I **record revenue** for jobs completed this week (or note what is missing)?  
- Did any **pricing Exception** happen? Is it written with an end date?  
- Are **receipts or basic records** missing that I should file?  
- Is there a **cash question** to review later in Module 5 — not solve now?

Do **not** use this block for:
- Actual-margin deep analysis  
- Cash-flow planning  
- Runway calculations  
- Full Core Number Set review  
- Redesigning Target Margin or Guardrails  

If a price needs recalculating, open the **Pricing & Margin Calculator** (Frozen V1.0) or your Module 3 sheets — then return to the week. Do not turn Wednesday into finance class.

Write checkpoint answers in one short line each. “Invoice missing for Thursday job.” “One Exception ends Friday.” “Cash question parked for Module 5.” That is enough. Long paragraphs here usually mean you started analyzing.

> **WARNING**
> If a customer pushes for a discount during the week, do not invent a new price in the hallway. Use your Discount Guardrails. If you grant an Exception, write it down. Panic discounts are how a good pricing system dies quietly.

> **EXAMPLE — Maya's Property Services**
> In her Wednesday Pipeline and Follow-Up Block (~45 minutes), Maya:
> - Moves a property manager to **Proposed** with Core turnovers at her priced rate (from Module 3).  
> - Sends one Follow-Up Cadence message due that week.  
> - Runs Should I Say Yes? on a same-week inquiry; decision **Not now** (capacity) — stays in Lead with a revisit date.  
> - Pricing Checkpoint: no Exceptions; one invoice still to record — noted for Close.  
>
> She does not recalculate her Cost Stack mid-block. *(Illustrative example only. No real client data.)*

**Exercise 4.4 — Schedule the Sell block and draft your checkpoint**

1. Confirm the calendar window for your Pipeline and Follow-Up Block (30–60 min).  
2. Copy this Money and Pricing Checkpoint checklist into your notes:
   - [ ] Revenue recorded / gaps noted  
   - [ ] Pricing Exceptions reviewed (or none)  
   - [ ] Receipts/records missing?  
   - [ ] Cash question to park for Module 5?  
3. Run one sample pass on your real pipeline this week (even 20 minutes counts as practice).

That scheduled block + checklist is your Chapter 4.4 output.

## Chapter 4.5 — The Weekly Close

The week does not end when the last job ends. It ends when you **Close**.

Without a Close, unfinished follow-ups, fuzzy prices, and half-remembered problems leak into the next week. With a Close, you tell the truth once, briefly, and hand yourself a clean starting point.

### Framework: The Weekly Close

**Duration:** 25–45 minutes in a normal week. **Hard ceiling: 45 minutes.**  
In a consolidation week or catch-up week only, you may stretch to **60 minutes** — then return to the ceiling.

If your Close regularly needs more than 45 minutes, the Close is too big. Cut questions. Do not “get stronger” by making the ritual longer.

**Weekly Close agenda (stay inside the ceiling):**

1. **What shipped?** (5 min) — jobs delivered; anything redone.  
2. **Pipeline truth** (8–10 min) — stages updated; next actions real.  
3. **Follow-ups** (5 min) — what is due; what moved to Lost cleanly.  
4. **Money and pricing notice** (5 min) — same light questions as the checkpoint; no Module 5 analysis.  
5. **One lesson** (3 min) — one sentence only.  
6. **Next-Week Handoff** (5 min) — see below.  
7. **Stop.**

That is enough. A Close is a ritual, not a board meeting.

### Keep the Close inside 45 minutes

Use this simple timing map in a normal week:

| Segment | Minutes |
|---|---|
| What shipped | 5 |
| Pipeline truth | 8–10 |
| Follow-ups | 5 |
| Money/pricing notice | 5 |
| One lesson | 3 |
| Next-Week Handoff | 5 |
| Buffer / stop | remaining time up to 45 |

If you are still rewriting your whole offer at minute 40, you have left the Close. Park that work for a Planning Block or a later module. The Close earns its power by ending.

### Framework: Next-Week Handoff

The Close must not end only by looking backward. Finish with a brief **Next-Week Handoff** — four lines that set up Planning without *doing* Planning.

Write only:

1. **Next week’s top priority** (one outcome).  
2. **First unresolved follow-up** (who/what — no novel).  
3. **First delivery risk or capacity concern**.  
4. **Date/window of the next Weekly Planning Block**.

Then stop. Do not build the full top-3 list, the full calendar, or a new offer strategy here. That belongs in the Planning Block. The Handoff exists so next week does not start from zero.

### Handoff versus Planning — keep the seam clean

| Next-Week Handoff (in Close) | Weekly Planning Block (next window) |
|---|---|
| One top priority | Full top 3 outcomes |
| First unresolved follow-up | Full pipeline pass if needed |
| First delivery/capacity risk | Full capacity check |
| Next Planning date/window | Confirms and runs the whole Planning agenda |

If your Handoff grows past four short lines, you are Planning early. Move the extra work to the Planning Block. Protecting this seam keeps Close inside 45 minutes.

> **TIP**
> Set a timer for 40 minutes when you Close. When it rings, finish the Handoff and quit. Incomplete honesty on time beats a perfect Close you postpone until never.

> **EXAMPLE — Maya's Property Services**
> Friday Close (~35 minutes). Maya notes eight turnovers delivered; one supply shortage flagged with her worker; pipeline updated; one proposal waiting; revenue mostly recorded, one invoice still open (noticed, not analyzed).  
>
> **Next-Week Handoff:**
> 1. Top priority: send the waiting Proposed follow-up and protect Wednesday’s Sell block.  
> 2. First unresolved follow-up: property manager proposal — cadence day 3.  
> 3. Delivery risk: two back-to-back turnovers Thursday morning — confirm worker start time.  
> 4. Next Planning Block: Sunday evening, ~30 minutes.  
>
> She does not rebuild the whole week in the Close. *(Illustrative example only.)*

1. Work the agenda above inside 45 minutes.  
2. Fill all four Next-Week Handoff lines.  
3. Write the time you finished: ____

## Chapter 4.6 — Protecting and Recovering the Rhythm

A rhythm that only works on perfect weeks is not a rhythm. It is a wish. This chapter installs two tools: **Rhythm Protection Rules** and a **Catch-Up Protocol**.

### Framework: Rhythm Protection Rules

Write 4–6 rules that protect your blocks without pretending emergencies never happen. Keep them plain.

Starter set (adapt freely):

1. **Planning and Close are protected.** I may shorten them; I do not delete both in the same week without using Catch-Up.  
2. **The Pipeline and Follow-Up Block can move; it cannot vanish for two weeks in a row.**  
3. **Daily Anchors stay at 2–3.** I do not “improve” them into a long checklist.  
4. **Emergencies reschedule Deliver/Sell blocks — they do not permanently rewrite the rhythm.**  
5. **I do not negotiate prices when exhausted.** Guardrails still apply after a hard day.  
6. **Consolidation weeks (in the 13-week journey: Weeks 4, 8, 12) are lighter on purpose.** I protect Close and catch up; I do not pile on new systems.

Rules are for you, not for performance theater. If a rule is unrealistic, rewrite it until you will actually keep it.

### Framework: Catch-Up Protocol

When the week breaks — urgent job, sick day, vehicle issue, family need — use a short protocol. Disruption is normal. Silence afterward is what creates drift.

**Minimum Catch-Up (15–25 minutes mid-disruption, or same day):**
1. Name what broke (one line).  
2. Protect life and delivery first.  
3. Move — do not cancel forever — the missed Planning or Sell block.  
4. Keep Daily Anchors if possible; if not, resume tomorrow.  

**End-of-week Catch-Up Close (up to 60 minutes only in catch-up/consolidation weeks):**
1. Run a shortened Close.  
2. Complete the Next-Week Handoff.  
3. Rebook the next Planning Block.  
4. Write one sentence: what I will protect first next week.

Catch-Up is professionalism. It is not a confession.

### Protect rest on purpose

A sustainable rhythm includes recovery. That is not a soft extra — it is how you still have a business next year. Your Protection Rules may include a rest boundary (for example: no Pipeline block after a 12-hour delivery day; move it 24 hours instead of skipping sleep). Health, family, and rest are part of capacity. A week that only works by borrowing from them is not a week you can repeat.

### Consolidation weeks

In the BBOS 13-week journey, Weeks 4, 8, and 12 are lighter consolidation weeks. Use them to stabilize the rhythm, not to invent new rituals. Keep Planning + Close. Shorten Pipeline/Follow-Up if needed. Do not add Module 5 work early “because you have time.”

> **EXAMPLE — Maya's Property Services**
> Midweek, an urgent same-day turnover lands during Maya’s Pipeline and Follow-Up Block. She takes the job (capacity allows), stops the Sell block at minute 20, and writes: “Catch-Up: Friday 20 min before Close — finish follow-ups.”  
>
> Friday she runs Catch-Up, then a normal-length Close with Handoff. She does not call the week a failure. She calls it a week with a protocol. *(Illustrative example only.)*

**Exercise 4.6 — Write your protection kit**

1. List **4–6 Rhythm Protection Rules**.  
2. Write your **Catch-Up Protocol** in 5–8 lines (minimum midweek steps + end-of-week Close steps).  
3. Circle the one rule you are most likely to break — and rewrite it to be more realistic.

That kit is your Chapter 4.6 output.

### Common rhythm mistakes (and the fix)

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Hour-by-hour perfect calendar | Use blocks and 2–3 anchors only |
| Ten Daily Anchors | Cap at 2–3 |
| Close becomes a 2-hour life review | Timer + 45-minute ceiling + brief Handoff |
| Skipping Sell block for three weeks | Protection Rule: it may move, not vanish twice |
| Rebuilding offers inside Planning | Planning uses Snapshot; redesign waits |
| Analyzing runway in the Pricing Checkpoint | Notice only; Module 5 analyzes |
| Treating disruption as proof you “can’t keep a system” | Use Catch-Up; resume |

The goal is not a perfect week. The goal is a week you can steer.

## Maya’s week — one full illustrative pass

*Illustrative example in a fictional week for Maya's Property Services. Not real schedules, clients, addresses, access codes, or business data.*

Maya’s week is mostly delivery — as it should be. The rhythm does not replace turnovers. It keeps Selling and Closing from disappearing between them.

**Sunday evening (~30 minutes) — Planning Block.**  
Maya opens her Snapshot for one minute, then checks capacity: turnovers already booked, her part-time worker’s available hours, and her own limit after a heavy prior week. Top 3 outcomes: (1) deliver booked turnovers to standard, (2) send one priced proposal from a Qualified opportunity, (3) protect Wednesday’s Pipeline and Follow-Up Block. She notes Thursday morning looks tight. She confirms Friday Close still fits.

**Monday–Tuesday — Deliver + Daily Anchors.**  
She runs turnovers. Anchors stay small: confirm next-day jobs and access notes with her worker; one short pipeline glance; quality tick after each job (guest-ready standard met? supply note?). No attempt to “build the business” for an hour each dawn.

**Wednesday (~45 minutes) — Pipeline and Follow-Up Block.**  
She updates stages (~12 min), sends one cadence follow-up that is due (~8 min), and spends ~10 minutes on **proposal prep**: a specific Core rung at her Module 3 price, Price-Hold Script ready, no new discount invented. She runs Should I Say Yes? on a new inquiry — **Not now** for capacity — and leaves it in Lead with a revisit date. **Pricing checkpoint (~7 min):** no Exceptions; one completed job still needs revenue recorded; no receipt gap; one cash question parked for later — noticed for Close, not analyzed.

**Wednesday later — Disruption.**  
An urgent same-day turnover request lands. Capacity allows it. Maya pauses the remaining Sell tasks, takes the job, and writes a Catch-Up note: finish follow-ups Friday before Close. She does not scrap the rhythm.

**Thursday — Delivery and coordination.**  
Back-to-back morning turnovers. She coordinates start time and supplies with her worker in a short handoff, then runs quality ticks. The day is heavy. She does not also force a second Pipeline block at night.

**Friday — Catch-Up + Weekly Close.**  
She spends ~20 minutes finishing the paused follow-ups (Catch-Up), then runs Close in ~35 minutes inside the normal ceiling: what shipped; pipeline truth; follow-ups; money notice (record that invoice); one lesson (“urgent jobs are fine if Catch-Up is written”). Total Friday system time is longer because Catch-Up sits beside Close — the Close itself still respects 45 minutes.  

**Next-Week Handoff (four lines only):**  
1. Top priority — protect Wednesday Sell block and complete the waiting proposal follow-up.  
2. First unresolved follow-up — property manager proposal, cadence day 3.  
3. Delivery risk — Thursday morning load; confirm worker start time early.  
4. Next Planning Block — Sunday evening, ~30 minutes.  

This is a realistic week: mostly delivery, small protected system blocks, one disruption, recovery without drama.
