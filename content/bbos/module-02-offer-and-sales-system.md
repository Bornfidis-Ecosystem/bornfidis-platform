## Module purpose

In Module 1 you saw your business clearly. You named the business you are really in, your ideal customer, and your core offer, and you wrote them onto your One-Page Business Snapshot. Module 2 takes those pieces and turns them into a working sales system.

Right now, for most owners, selling is a scramble. A service list lives half in your head and half in old messages. You quote from memory. Leads arrive, get a reply, and then vanish because nothing tracks them. Some good opportunities go cold simply because you forgot they existed.

Module 2 replaces that scramble with structure. You will package your core offer so it can be sold and delivered the same way every time. You will build a simple ladder around it, put it on one clean page, and set up a visible pipeline that shows you every live opportunity and what to do next. You will also install two habits that protect your time: a clear way to decide which work to accept, and a steady rhythm of following up so nothing slips.

One thing this module is **not**: it is not about price. Module 2 defines *what* you sell and *how you track and follow up on opportunities*. Setting the actual numbers — what each offer costs and what you charge — is the job of Module 3 (Pricing With Dignity). Keeping those two apart is deliberate. Structure first, then price. Trying to price a fuzzy offer is how owners end up guessing, which is exactly the pattern you are here to end.

## Chapter 2.1 — From Services to a Structured Offer

Ask ten small service businesses what they sell and most will read you a list. "I do turnovers, regular cleans, one-off deep cleans, sometimes organizing, and I can help with small errands." A list is not an offer. It is a menu with no shape, and it creates three problems: customers cannot tell what you are best at, you cannot deliver it the same way twice, and — the problem for later — you cannot price it cleanly.

A **structured offer** fixes this. It takes the core offer you named in Module 1 and defines it precisely enough that you could hand it to someone else and they would know exactly what is promised. The tool for this is the **Offer Builder**.

### Why structure comes before everything else

It is worth understanding why this chapter comes first, because it shapes the rest of the module and the rest of BBOS. A vague offer quietly breaks every system you try to build on top of it.

- You **cannot price** a fuzzy offer. If "a clean" means something different every time, every quote is a guess — the exact pattern Module 3 exists to end.
- You **cannot track** a fuzzy offer. Your pipeline needs to know what each opportunity is *for*; "some kind of work, maybe" is impossible to move through stages.
- You **cannot delegate** a fuzzy offer. If the offer only exists in your head, only you can deliver it — which keeps you the bottleneck you named in Module 1.
- You **cannot follow up** well on a fuzzy offer, because you are never quite sure what you are following up about.

Defining your offer clearly is not paperwork. It is the load-bearing wall of your whole sales system. Get it right here and the next four chapters become straightforward. Skip it, and every later step wobbles.

### From a menu to a promise

The shift you are making in this chapter is from a *menu* to a *promise*. A menu says "here are things I might do." A promise says "here is exactly what you will get, every time." Customers trust promises. They can compare a promise to what they actually receive, and when the two match, you have earned repeat work and referrals without asking for them. A menu gives them nothing to hold you to — and nothing to remember you for.

### Framework: The Offer Builder

The Offer Builder has 5 fields. Fill them in for your core offer. Keep each field short and specific — you are defining a product, not writing a brochure.

1. **Who it is for** — your ideal customer from Module 1. Naming this keeps the offer focused. An offer built for everyone fits no one.
2. **Intended outcome** — the real result the customer gets. Not the task ("cleaning") but the outcome ("a home that is consistently guest-ready"). This comes straight from the "business you are really in" line on your Snapshot.
3. **Included** — everything that always comes with this offer, every time, at the standard price. This is your promise. If it is on this list, the customer never has to ask for it.
4. **Optional** — named add-ons the customer can choose. These are real extras, not vague "we can also do more." **Leave them unpriced here** — each optional item becomes its own price line in Module 3. Naming them now means you never have to invent an add-on on the spot.
5. **Not Included** — what this offer explicitly does *not* cover. This is the most-skipped and most-valuable field. Clear exclusions prevent scope creep, protect your time, and set honest expectations before the work starts.

Together, **Included / Optional / Not Included** are the boundaries of your offer. Boundaries are not unfriendly. They are how you keep every job predictable and every customer clear on what they are getting.

> **TIP**
> If you find yourself wanting to list ten things under Included, you are probably describing three different offers. Pick the one core offer for now. You will add an entry and premium version in the next chapter.

> **WARNING**
> The most expensive words in a small service business are "sure, I can also do that." Every unplanned yes stretches your scope, your time, and your delivery standard. The Not Included field exists so you can say, kindly and clearly, "that is outside this offer, but here is what I can do."

> **EXAMPLE — Maya's Property Services**
> Maya packages her core turnover clean with the Offer Builder *(fictional example)*:
> - **Who it is for:** short-term rental hosts and small property managers with 2–5 properties, plus homeowners who need dependable, on-time cleaning.
> - **Intended outcome:** a rental that is consistently guest-ready on time, so the host never scrambles before a check-in.
> - **Included:** full turnover clean (kitchen, bathrooms, floors, surfaces), fresh linen reset, trash removal, and a guest-ready walkthrough check.
> - **Optional:** inside-oven and inside-fridge deep clean, laundry service, and consumables restock (coffee, paper goods). *Prices set in Module 3.*
> - **Not Included:** repairs and maintenance, moving heavy furniture, and any outdoor or yard work.
>
> Notice Maya has not mentioned a single price. She has defined *what the offer is* — the number comes later. *(Example only.)*

## Chapter 2.2 — The Offer Ladder

One offer is a strong start. But most customers are not all ready for the same thing. Some want to test you before committing. Some want exactly your core offer. A few want more — a bigger, ongoing, or more complete version. If you only sell one thing, you either lose the cautious customer or overwhelm the eager one.

The **Offer Ladder** solves this without turning you into a business with a hundred products. It is three clear rungs built around the single core offer you just structured. It gives customers a way to start small and a way to grow with you — while you keep your delivery simple.

### Framework: The Offer Ladder

Your ladder has exactly 3 rungs. For each rung, capture two things: **what it is** and **who it suits**.

1. **Entry** — a low-commitment first step. This is the easiest way for a new customer to try you with little risk. It is smaller in scope than your core offer, and it exists to build trust and open the door. *Who it suits:* cautious first-time customers.
2. **Core** — the main offer you structured in Chapter 2.1. This is what you want to sell most often. It is the center of your business and the rung everything else supports. *Who it suits:* customers who already know what they need.
3. **Premium** — a fuller, larger, or recurring version for customers who want more. This might mean more frequency, more properties, more scope, or an ongoing arrangement. *Who it suits:* your best-fit customers who value what you do and want more of it.

Every rung uses a **price placeholder**, never a number:

```text
[price — set in Module 3]
```

You are building the *shape* of the ladder now. In Module 3 you will price each rung with dignity, using the Cost Stack → Target Margin → Price method. Leaving placeholders keeps you honest: it stops you anchoring to a guessed number before you have done the costing work.

### How the ladder serves different customers

A ladder is not about selling more for its own sake. It is about meeting customers where they are, so the right people can say yes at the right level.

- The **cautious customer** who does not know you yet can start on the entry rung. The risk to them is small, so the decision is easy. If you deliver well, trust grows and they climb.
- The **ready customer** who already knows what they need goes straight to your core offer. You do not have to talk them up or down — the core rung is exactly right.
- The **best-fit customer** who values what you do and wants more has somewhere to go. Without a premium rung, these customers quietly cap out at your core offer even when they would happily commit to more.

A single offer forces every customer through the same narrow door. A ladder gives three doors — while your delivery stays built around one clear core.

> **TIP**
> Do not over-build the ladder — 3 rungs is enough for Version 1. If you find yourself designing five or six, you are adding complexity you will have to price, deliver, and explain. And keep the rungs genuinely different, not "small, medium, large" versions of the same thing: the entry rung should be an easy first step; the premium rung should serve a clearly different, deeper need.

> **WARNING**
> A ladder is for the customer's benefit, not a pressure tactic. The goal is to meet people where they are, not to push everyone to the top rung. If a customer's best fit is the entry rung, that is a win — trust now often becomes core work later.

> **EXAMPLE — Maya's Property Services**
> Maya builds her 3-rung ladder around her turnover clean *(fictional example)*:
> - **Entry:** a one-time trial turnover for a new host — a single clean so they can see her standard before committing. *Who it suits:* cautious first-time hosts. `[price — set in Module 3]`
> - **Core:** scheduled turnover cleans, booked around the host's check-in calendar. *Who it suits:* hosts with a regular rental. `[price — set in Module 3]`
> - **Premium:** recurring multi-property support for hosts or property managers with several units — regular turnovers plus priority scheduling. *Who it suits:* multi-property managers. `[price — set in Module 3]`
>
> Each rung is a real, different level of commitment. None of them has a price yet — that is Module 3's job. *(Example only.)*

**Exercise 2.2 — Build your Offer Ladder**

Define your 3 rungs — Entry, Core, Premium — around your structured offer. Write one or two lines for each rung describing what it is and who it suits. Use `[price — set in Module 3]` on every rung. Do not add numbers.

## Chapter 2.3 — The Offer One-Pager

You now have a structured offer and a ladder. But they are only useful if a customer can understand them quickly. Most buying decisions for small services happen fast, often on a phone, often while the person is busy. If your offer takes a paragraph of explanation, you lose them.

The **Offer One-Pager** is the answer. It is a single page that says, clearly and calmly: here is what I do, here is who it is for, here is what you get, and here is how to start. It is the thing you send when someone asks "what do you offer?" — instead of typing a different reply every time.

### Framework: The Offer One-Pager

Your one-pager pulls directly from the work you have already done. It has 6 short parts:

1. **Headline** — one line that states the outcome you deliver, drawn from your "business you are really in" sentence. Not "cleaning services" but the result the customer wants.
2. **Who it is for** — a short line naming your ideal customer so the right people recognize themselves.
3. **What you get (Included)** — the Included list from your Offer Builder, as clear bullets.
4. **Optional add-ons** — your Optional items, named (no prices).
5. **The ladder** — a short mention of the entry, core, and premium options, so customers see a way to start and a way to grow.
6. **How to start** — one clear next step. What should the customer do right now? Reply, call, book a first clean? Make it a single, obvious action.

The one-pager is a **communication tool, not a contract**. Keep it plain, warm, and specific. It should be readable in under a minute and understandable by someone who has never met you.

### What makes a one-pager actually work

Most owners' descriptions of their business fail in the same way: they describe the *tasks* ("I do cleaning, laundry, and errands") instead of the *outcome* ("your rental is guest-ready, every time"). The task list makes the customer do the work of imagining the result. The outcome does that work for them.

Three things separate a one-pager that converts interest into action from one that gets ignored:

1. **It leads with the outcome, not the activity.** The headline answers "what do I get?" before "what do you do?"
2. **It is specific enough to be believed.** Vague claims ("high-quality, reliable service") sound like everyone else. Concrete inclusions ("fresh linen reset and a guest-ready walkthrough check") sound like someone who actually knows the work.
3. **It ends with one obvious next step.** Confusion kills action. If the customer has to figure out how to start, many simply will not. One clear instruction removes that friction.

Everything on the page should earn its place. If a line does not help the customer understand the offer or take the next step, cut it.

> **NOTE**
> The Offer One-Pager is a BBOS companion asset. In this module you are drafting its content. The final designed file follows the Style Guide's one-pager layout; you do not need to design it here — you need the words right.

> **WARNING**
> Do not put prices on your one-pager yet. Until Module 3 sets your prices with dignity, a number here would be a guess — and a guessed price is hard to walk back once a customer has seen it.

> **EXAMPLE — Maya's Property Services**
> Maya drafts her Offer One-Pager *(fictional example)*:
> - **Headline:** "Turnovers that leave your rental guest-ready, every time — so you never scramble before a check-in."
> - **Who it is for:** short-term rental hosts and small property managers with 2–5 properties, and homeowners who want a dependable clean.
> - **What you get:** full turnover clean, fresh linen reset, trash removal, and a guest-ready walkthrough check — done to the same standard every visit.
> - **Optional add-ons:** inside-oven/fridge deep clean, laundry service, consumables restock.
> - **Options:** start with a one-time trial turnover, move to scheduled turnovers, or set up recurring multi-property support.
> - **How to start:** "Send me your next check-out date and I'll confirm your first turnover."
>
> Clear, calm, and price-free. A busy host can read it in under a minute and know exactly what to do next. *(Example only.)*

**Exercise 2.3 — Write your Offer One-Pager**

Draft all 6 parts of your one-pager, pulling from your Offer Builder and Offer Ladder. Keep it to one page and under a minute to read. End with a single, clear "how to start" action. This completed one-pager is your Chapter 2.3 output.

> **DO THIS NOW**
> Send your draft one-pager to one person who fits your ideal customer, or read it to them. Ask: "Is it clear what I do and how to start?" If they hesitate, simplify — do not add more words.

## Chapter 2.4 — The Visible Pipeline and Qualifying

You have an offer worth selling. Now you need to stop losing the chances to sell it. In most small businesses, opportunities live in scattered places: a text here, a voicemail there, a "I'll circle back" that never happens. What you cannot see, you cannot act on — and invisible opportunities quietly become lost ones.

A **pipeline** makes every opportunity visible in one place, sorted by how close it is to becoming paid work. It is not software you have to buy. It is a simple board or list — on paper, a whiteboard, or a spreadsheet — that answers one question at a glance: *where does each opportunity stand, and what is the next action?*

### Framework: The Visible Pipeline

BBOS uses these exact stages, in this order:

```text
Lead → Qualified → Proposed → Won / Lost
```

Each opportunity sits in exactly one stage. An opportunity moves forward only when it passes that stage's **entry test** — a simple, honest check that it truly belongs in the next stage. Entry tests stop your pipeline from filling with wishful thinking.

- **Lead** — someone has shown interest, but you have not yet decided whether to pursue them. *Entry test:* a real person or business has made contact or been identified as a possible customer.
- **Qualified** — you have decided this is a real, good-fit opportunity worth your time. *Entry test:* the opportunity passes the **Should I Say Yes?** filter with a **Yes** (see below).
- **Proposed** — the customer has received a specific Offer Ladder rung at a defined price and you are awaiting their decision. *Entry test:* a specific rung at a defined price has been presented (priced with Cost Stack → Target Margin → Price from Module 3).
- **Won** — they have agreed and confirmed the work. *Entry test:* a clear yes and a booked or scheduled commitment.
- **Lost** — they declined, went silent past your follow-up window, or turned out not to be a fit. *Entry test:* a clear no, or your follow-up attempts have run their course.

Won and Lost are both *closes* — they end an opportunity's journey. Losing is not a failure; a clean Lost is far better than an opportunity that lingers forever draining your attention.

A **"Not now" opportunity stays in the Lead stage with a scheduled revisit date.** It is not Qualified (you have not said Yes yet) and it is not Lost (you have not ruled it out). It simply waits in Lead until its revisit date, when you decide again.

> **NOTE**
> A pipeline is a visibility tool, not a forecast. Do not try to total up what your pipeline is "worth" — that would be a revenue guess, and BBOS does not deal in projected income. The pipeline's only job is to show you what is live and what to do next.

### Framework: Should I Say Yes? (the Lead → Qualified gate)

Not every lead deserves your time. The fastest way to protect your business is to decide, deliberately, which opportunities to pursue — before they consume hours. The **Should I Say Yes?** filter is a quick check you run on each lead at the moment you consider qualifying it. It has 5 checks:

1. **Fit** — is this my ideal customer, and does the request match my core offer or ladder? *(Pull this straight from your Module 1 ideal customer and your structured offer.)*
2. **Boundary** — is what they are asking for Included, Optional, or Not Included? A request that is mostly Not Included is a warning sign.
3. **Capacity** — can I take this on and deliver it well without harming the commitments I already have? Saying yes to work you cannot deliver well costs you more than saying no.
4. **Margin-viable** — can this be delivered inside my offer in a way that could be priced to a healthy margin? This is a **yes/no feasibility check only** — you are not calculating anything. You are asking, "Is this the kind of work that can pay properly?" The actual pricing happens in Module 3.
5. **Red flags** — are there poor-fit signals? Chronic pressure for discounts, chaos, disrespect for your time, or endless scope creep. One or two small flags may be fine; a pile of them is a clear signal.

Based on the checks, record **one decision** with **a one-line reason**. Use this simple rule to turn the 5 checks into a decision:

- **No** — the opportunity fails **Fit** or **Margin-viable**, or it carries several red flags. It is not right for your business. Decline respectfully. *(Reason example: "Mostly Not-Included work and heavy discount pressure.")*
- **Not now** — it passes Fit, Boundary, and Margin-viable, but **Capacity** or timing is wrong right now. It is a good fit at the wrong moment. Park it with a revisit date. *(Reason example: "Good fit but I'm at capacity until next month.")*
- **Yes** — it passes all 5 checks. Pursue it and move it to Qualified. *(Reason example: "Ideal customer, matches core offer, clear scope.")*

In short: a failed Fit or Margin-viable check (or a stack of red flags) is a **No**; a good fit blocked only by capacity or timing is a **Not now**; a clean pass on all five is a **Yes**. The one-line reason matters — it turns a gut feeling into a decision you can review later, and it makes it easy to be consistent and fair.

### Working your pipeline

A pipeline is only useful if you *work* it — if you look at it regularly and move each opportunity forward or close it out. A pipeline you set up once and never revisit becomes just another list of things you feel guilty about.

The discipline is simple: every live opportunity should always have a **next action** and a **stage**. When you look at your pipeline, you should be able to see, for each opportunity, what happens next and who is responsible for it (usually you, for now). If an opportunity has no next action, either give it one or close it to Lost. An opportunity with no next action is not really alive — it is just waiting to go cold.

This is where Module 2 connects to Module 4. In **Module 4 (Weekly Operating Rhythm)**, your pipeline review becomes part of **the Weekly Close** — a short, regular moment each week when you update every opportunity's stage and next action. You do not need to build that habit yet. For now, simply know that the pipeline you create here is designed to be worked weekly, not admired once. Setting it up is Module 2; running it every week is Module 4.

> **TIP**
> Keep the pipeline where you will actually see it — the same place you keep your One-Page Business Snapshot. A pipeline hidden in a file you never open cannot do its job. Visibility is the entire point.

To keep it simple to run, your Pipeline Tracker uses 6 columns:

**Opportunity · Stage · Decision (Yes / Not now / No) · One-line reason · Next action · Next follow-up / revisit date**

> **EXAMPLE — Maya's Property Services**

> | Property manager, 3 rentals | Qualified | Yes | Ideal customer, recurring | Send scope for weekly turnovers | Mon |

> **WARNING**
> "Not now" is not a polite way of avoiding a decision forever. Every "Not now" must carry a revisit date. Without one, it becomes a quiet "No" that the customer never heard — and a lingering maybe in your head.

## Chapter 2.5 — The Follow-Up Cadence

Here is a hard truth most owners learn the expensive way: many opportunities are lost not to a competitor but to silence. A lead replies with interest, you get busy, days pass, and the moment cools. The work was there. The follow-up was not.

A **Follow-Up Cadence** removes the guesswork and the guilt from staying in touch. It is a small set of rules that tells you exactly when and how to follow up, so you never have to wonder "should I message them again?" — the system already decided. Following up stops being nagging and becomes a normal, professional part of how you work.

### Framework: The Follow-Up Cadence

Your cadence uses one simple structure:

```text
Trigger → Action → Timing
```

- **Trigger** — the event that starts a follow-up. (A new lead arrives; a proposal has been sent; a customer has gone quiet.)
- **Action** — what you actually do. (Send a short intro and one qualifying question; check in on the proposal; send a friendly final nudge.)
- **Timing** — when you do it, and when you stop. (Within 24 hours; after 3 days; a final follow-up after 7 days, then move to Lost.)

Optionally, add a fourth column — **Pipeline stage** — so each rule is tied to where the opportunity sits (Lead, Qualified, or Proposed). This keeps your cadence and your pipeline pointing at the same opportunities.

Write a few of these rules — enough to cover your common situations, not so many that you cannot keep them. The power is in the *timing*, especially knowing when to stop. A cadence that never ends becomes pestering; a cadence with a clear final step keeps you respectful and lets you close opportunities cleanly to Lost.

Your Follow-Up Cadence applies to **active leads and current opportunities only** — the people currently moving (or stalling) in your pipeline.

### Why timing beats effort

Owners often think follow-up is about trying harder — sending more messages, being more persuasive. It is not. It is about being *reliable and timely*. A single well-timed message often does more than five anxious ones.

The reason is simple: people are busy, not uninterested. A lead who does not reply is usually not saying no — they are distracted, comparing options, or waiting for a better moment. A calm follow-up at the right time gives them an easy way back in. That is why the cadence is built around timing, not intensity.

Knowing when to **stop** is just as important as knowing when to start. A clear final message does two things: it respects the other person by not pestering them, and it lets *you* close the opportunity cleanly to Lost instead of carrying it around as an open loop. Open loops are exhausting. A cadence with a defined ending replaces "I should probably message them again" — a thought that drains you all week — with "the system handled it."

### Keep the cadence simple enough to run

The best Follow-Up Cadence is the one you will actually use. A complicated set of rules with a dozen branches looks thorough but never gets run, because you cannot hold it in your head during a busy week. Three or four clear rules that cover a new lead, a sent proposal, and a final nudge will carry almost every opportunity you have. Start small; you can always refine it once it is a habit.

> **NOTE**
> Following up with **past customers** — asking for repeat work, requesting referrals, or reactivating people who have gone quiet for a long time — is a different system with a different purpose. That belongs to **Module 7 (Reputation and Customer Retention)**. Keep Module 2's cadence focused on live opportunities so it stays simple and you actually run it.

> **WARNING**
> Done well, following up is a service, not a nuisance. Each message should be short, useful, and easy to say no to. A good follow-up gives the person a reason to reply, not a reason to feel pressured. If a cadence ends in a clean final message, you protect both the relationship and your own time.

> **EXAMPLE — Maya's Property Services**
> Maya writes her Follow-Up Cadence for active opportunities *(fictional example)*:
> - **Trigger:** new lead arrives → **Action:** reply with a short intro and one question ("What's your next check-out date?") → **Timing:** within 24 hours. *(Stage: Lead)*
> - **Trigger:** proposal/scope sent, no reply → **Action:** friendly check-in offering to answer questions → **Timing:** after 3 days. *(Stage: Proposed)*
> - **Trigger:** still no reply → **Action:** short final message ("Happy to help whenever you're ready") → **Timing:** after 7 days, then move to Lost. *(Stage: Proposed)*
>
> Now Maya never wonders whether to follow up. The cadence decides, and stalled opportunities close cleanly instead of haunting her to-do list. *(Example only.)*
