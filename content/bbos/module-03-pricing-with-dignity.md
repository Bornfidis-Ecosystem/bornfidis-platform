## Chapter 3.1 — Why Owners Under-Price

Under-pricing rarely starts as a strategy. It starts as a feeling: *If I ask for too much, I will lose the job.* So the owner lowers the number before the customer even asks. Or they set a price that "sounds reasonable" without knowing whether it covers the real cost of the work.

That pattern is expensive. An under-priced job does not just earn less — it fills your calendar with work that cannot sustain the business. You stay busy. You stay tired. And you still wonder why the account never feels solid.

### The common patterns

Owners under-price for a few familiar reasons:

1. **Fear of losing the sale.** Silence after a price feels like rejection, so they cut before the customer decides.
2. **Guessing.** They quote from memory because they have never written down what the job actually costs.
3. **Competitor copying.** They match someone else's number without knowing that person's costs, quality, or whether that person is even profitable.
4. **Apologizing for the price.** They state the number softly, then rush to discount before the customer has answered.
5. **Not counting their own time.** If the owner's hours are "free," every price looks high — and every job looks affordable when it is not.

None of these mean you are bad at business. They mean you have been pricing without a system. Module 3 installs the system.

### What under-pricing actually costs

Under-pricing is not only "less money on one invoice." It reshapes the whole week:

- **You attract the wrong work.** Discount-seeking customers often bring more chaos, more scope creep, and more pressure — the same red flags you named in Module 2.
- **You have no room to recover.** When a job runs long, a redo is needed, or supplies cost more than expected, a thin price turns a normal week into a loss.
- **You train customers to push.** If your first number always softens, the market learns that your price is a starting point, not a decision.
- **You delay every other system.** It is hard to hire, document SOPs, or plan cash when every job's economics are a mystery.

Pricing with dignity is not about charging "as much as possible." It is about charging enough, on purpose, so the business can keep its promises.

> **WARNING**
> Competitor prices are not your Cost Stack. Another business may under-price on purpose, ignore their own labor, or deliver a different standard. Use your own costs and your chosen margin first. Competitor awareness is a final reality check only — never the main method.

## Chapter 3.2 — Build the Cost Stack

You cannot price with dignity until you know what the work costs. The **Cost Stack** is that number: the full planned cost of delivering one unit of your offer — for Maya, one standard turnover clean.

The Cost Stack has four layers. Keep them simple. This is planning clarity, not a full accounting system.

### Framework: The Cost Stack

1. **Direct costs** — materials and supplies consumed by the job (cleaning products, laundry for that turnover, trash bags, and similar).
2. **Labor** — time to deliver × labor cost per hour. **Your own time counts as a real cost.** If you skip this, you are pretending the business can run on unpaid hours forever.
3. **Overhead allocation** — a simple, rough share of running costs the business carries whether or not this job happens (fuel/transport, phone, basic insurance, small tools). Keep this light: one per-job amount you can explain in a sentence. Do not build a complex cost-accounting model.
4. **Contingency** — a small buffer for the unexpected (a redo, a longer-than-planned job, a supply surprise). It is an estimate, clearly labeled as such — not false precision.

```text
Cost Stack = Direct costs + Labor + Overhead allocation + Contingency
```

### Keep overhead simple on purpose

Owners often freeze on overhead because it sounds like accounting. In BBOS it is not. Pick one practical method and stick to it for Version 1:

- **Per-job amount:** "I allocate about $X to each core job for transport, phone, and basic running costs."
- **Or per-hour amount:** "I add about $Y of overhead for each labor hour on the job."

Do not split every bill across seventeen categories. Do not wait for perfect books before you price. A rough allocation you use every time is more valuable than a perfect model you never finish. You can refine later in Module 5 when you compare plan to reality.

Contingency works the same way. Choose a small buffer that reflects how often jobs overrun in *your* business — then convert it to money and add it to the stack. Label it as an estimate. False precision helps no one.

> **TIP**
> Round your inputs. A Cost Stack that pretends to be exact to the last cent creates false confidence. Honest, round estimates you will actually use beat precise numbers you abandon.

> **NOTE**
> Overhead and contingency are planning estimates. They are not tax categories, bookkeeping rules, or accounting advice. Use them to make better pricing decisions. For formal books and filings, use a qualified professional.

### Labor is not optional

Many owners price as if their time is free. That is how a "good rate" still leaves them exhausted and underpaid. In BBOS, labor includes:

- Hours to do the work.
- Reasonable prep and wrap-up time tied to the job.
- A labor cost per hour that reflects what that time is worth to keep the business sustainable — even when you are the one doing the work.

You are not inventing a fake expense. You are refusing to treat your life as an unlimited free input. If you have a part-time worker, include their paid time for the job as labor too. The Cost Stack should reflect who actually delivers the work.

## Chapter 3.3 — Choose a Target Margin

Cost alone is not a price. If you only cover cost, the business cannot absorb mistakes, slow months, or growth. **Margin** is the planned gap between price and cost — the part that keeps the business sustainable.

BBOS separates two related decisions. Do not treat them as the same number.

### Framework: Target Margin and Margin Guardrail

- **Target Margin** — the planned margin you want the offer to achieve in normal conditions. This is your aim when you calculate the standard price.
- **Margin Guardrail** — the lowest planned margin you will accept **without** a documented Exception Approval. Below this line, you do not improvise in the moment. You either hold, redesign the scope, or use the Exception Approval rule in Chapter 3.6.

Neither number is universal. BBOS does not prescribe a "correct" percentage for every business. You choose both deliberately, write them down, and use them consistently.

### How to choose (without pretending there is one right answer)

Ask:

1. **What margin keeps this work worth doing** at my real Cost Stack?
2. **What margin leaves room** for the unexpected without panic?
3. **What Margin Guardrail protects me** from fear-based discounts in the moment?

Write the reasons in plain language. A margin you can explain is a margin you can hold.

### Why two numbers beat one

If you only set a Target Margin, every discount request becomes a negotiation with yourself in real time. Under pressure, "aim for 30%" quietly becomes "maybe 15% is fine just this once."

The **Margin Guardrail** stops that slide. It is the line you do not cross in the moment. Crossing it — if you ever do — requires a written Exception Approval with a reason and an end date. That friction is the point. It turns fear into a conscious decision.

Typical relationship:

```text
Margin Guardrail  <  Target Margin
```

Example shape (not a prescription): you might aim for a healthier Target Margin day to day, and refuse to quote below a lower Guardrail without documentation. Your percentages will differ. What matters is that both are written, distinct, and used.

> **WARNING**
> Do not copy a stranger's margin percentage from the internet or a competitor and call it yours. Your Cost Stack, offer, and risk are your own. Choose numbers you can defend for *this* business.

### Margin-viable (from Module 2) now has a reference point

In Module 2, **Should I Say Yes?** asked whether work looked **Margin-viable** — a yes/no feasibility check, not a calculation. Now you have a real reference: if the work cannot be delivered inside your offer and still clear your Margin Guardrail, it is not Margin-viable. You still do not invent a discount to force a yes. You protect the guardrail.

## Chapter 3.4 — Calculate the Price

You now have a Cost Stack and a Target Margin. The BBOS pricing method turns them into a price.

### Framework: Cost Stack → Target Margin → Price

Use these formulas consistently:

```text
Price = Cost Stack ÷ (1 − Target Margin)
```

```text
Margin = (Price − Cost Stack) ÷ Price
```

In the first formula, Target Margin is a decimal (30% = 0.30). In the second, you verify that the price you got actually delivers the margin you planned.

### Work the formula in plain steps

If formulas feel intimidating, use this sequence every time:

1. Write your **Cost Stack** as one money amount.
2. Write your **Target Margin** as a percent, then as a decimal (25% → 0.25; 30% → 0.30).
3. Calculate **1 − Target Margin** (for 30%: 1 − 0.30 = 0.70).
4. Divide **Cost Stack ÷ that result** to get **Price**.
5. Verify with **(Price − Cost Stack) ÷ Price**. You should land back on your Target Margin (or extremely close, if you rounded).

If step 5 does not match, you either used markup by mistake, divided wrong, or mixed currencies/units. Fix the math before you quote the number.

### Margin is not markup

This is the most important distinction in the module.

- **Margin** is the profit portion of the *price*: `(Price − Cost) ÷ Price`.
- **Markup** is the amount added on top of *cost*: `(Price − Cost) ÷ Cost`.

They are not interchangeable. A 30% **margin** is not the same as a 30% **markup**.

Quick check with round numbers: if cost is $100 and you want a **30% margin**, price is $100 ÷ 0.70 ≈ $143 — not $130. Adding 30% on top of cost ($130) only yields about a **23% margin**. If you confuse the two, you will systematically under-price while believing you hit your target.

BBOS always prices to **margin**, then verifies with the margin formula.

> **WARNING**
> If a spreadsheet, habit, or old quote card uses markup language, translate it before you trust it. Module 3 and the Pricing & Margin Calculator use margin only.

## Chapter 3.5 — Price the Offer Ladder and Optional Items

Your Module 2 Offer Ladder still shows `[price — set in Module 3]` on every rung. Optional items are named and still unpriced. This chapter fills both — using the **same method**, not three different guessing games.

### Price each rung from its own Cost Stack

Entry, Core, and Premium are different scopes. Do not invent ladder prices by arbitrarily "taking 20% off Core" unless that discount still clears your method. The clean approach:

1. Build (or adjust) a Cost Stack for each rung's real scope.
2. Apply the same Target Margin (unless you deliberately choose otherwise and write why).
3. Calculate each price with `Cost Stack ÷ (1 − Target Margin)`.

### Price Optional items separately

Optional items are not free add-ons and not vague "a bit more." Each Optional item from your Offer Builder gets its own small Cost Stack and its own price at your Target Margin. That keeps scope creep from silently destroying margin.

A practical way to cost a small Optional item:

1. Direct costs for that add-on only.
2. Extra labor minutes converted to hours × your labor cost.
3. A tiny overhead touch if it meaningfully uses transport or supplies — or zero if it truly rides inside a job already allocated.
4. A small contingency only if the add-on often overruns.

Then run the same price formula. If an Optional item takes almost no extra cost, its price will be small — that is fine. If it takes real time, its price should show it. What you must not do is throw in "extra work" for free because you feel awkward naming it.

> **EXAMPLE — Maya's Property Services**
>
> *Illustrative example in USD. Replace the currency symbol and figures with your own local inputs.*
>
> **Offer Ladder prices** (Target Margin 30% throughout):

> | Rung | Cost Stack (summary) | Price |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Entry** — one-time trial turnover | Direct $12 + Labor $40 (2 hrs × $20) + Overhead $10 + Contingency $8 = **$70** | 70 ÷ 0.70 = **$100** |

### Not Included stays out of the Cost Stack

Your Module 2 **Not Included** list is a pricing tool as much as a scope tool. If a request sits in Not Included, do not quietly fold it into a Core price "to be helpful." Either:

- Decline that part of the work,
- Move it to a priced Optional item (if you decide to offer it), or
- Create a different offer rung later — still priced with the same method.

Free scope is unpaid Cost Stack. Boundaries protect margin.

## Chapter 3.6 — Present, Hold, and Protect the Price

A correct price still fails if you abandon it the moment the room goes quiet. This chapter installs three tools: a **Price-Hold Script**, **Discount Guardrails**, and an **Exception Approval** rule. Together they turn pricing from a feeling into a professional habit.

### Framework: The Price-Hold Script

Your script is short — enough to say calmly, then stop talking. Fill it in with your real offer and price:

1. **State the offer clearly** (rung + what is Included).
2. **State the price** in one clean sentence.
3. **Stop.** Let the customer respond. Do not rush to discount in the silence.
4. **If they hesitate,** clarify scope or timing — do not automatically cut the number.

Example shape (adapt to your voice):

> "For a scheduled turnover clean — full clean, linen reset, trash out, and guest-ready check — the price is $150 per turnover. I can hold [date] if that works for you."

Then wait.

> **TIP**
> Silence after a price is information, not an emergency. Many customers are thinking, not rejecting. Your job is to stay steady long enough for them to answer.

### Holding the price without becoming rigid

Holding a price is not the same as being rude. You can be warm and clear at the same time:

- **If the scope is wrong,** adjust the rung or the Optional items — then restate the matching price.
- **If timing is the issue,** offer a later slot rather than a lower number.
- **If the customer needs a smaller start,** move them to your Entry rung at its real price — do not invent a random discount on Core.
- **If they need more,** move them toward Premium or add priced Optional items.

That is pricing with dignity: the number stays connected to the offer. You are not punishing the customer, and you are not abandoning your Cost Stack.

### Framework: Discount Guardrails

Decide these **before** you are in a tense conversation:

1. **I do not quote below my Margin Guardrail** without Exception Approval.
2. **If I move on price, I trade for something real** — smaller scope, off-peak timing, prepay, or a longer commitment — not a naked cut.
3. **I do not match a vague "someone else is cheaper"** claim without knowing scope and standard.
4. **I would rather lose a poor-fit job than win one that breaks the business.**

Write your guardrails in your own words. Keep them where you can see them when you quote.

### Framework: Exception Approval

Conscious exceptions are allowed. Panic exceptions are not.

An **Exception Approval** is a written note that includes:

- The customer / opportunity (no private third-party financial details needed — a short label is enough).
- The normal price and the exception price.
- Proof the exception still respects your rules (or explicitly states if you are going below the Margin Guardrail — and why).
- The **reason**.
- The **end date** (exceptions expire).
- Your approval (for a solo owner: you, in writing).

Exceptions are logged and rare. They are not a second, secret price list.

### When an exception is wise — and when it is not

A bounded exception can make sense when:

- The customer is a strong long-term fit and you are buying a short, clear trial period.
- You are trading a temporary price for something valuable (confirmed schedule, prepaid block, off-peak timing).
- The exception price still clears your Margin Guardrail — or you have written an explicit reason for going below it and a hard end date.

An exception is usually unwise when:

- You are discounting only because silence felt uncomfortable.
- The customer has already shown red flags (chaos, disrespect, endless scope creep).
- There is no end date, no trade, and no written reason.
- You would be embarrassed to explain the exception to your future self in 90 days.

Dignity includes the courage to say no. It also includes the discipline to say yes *on purpose* when a short, clean exception truly serves the business.

> **WARNING**
> An exception without an end date becomes your new default price. Bound it, or do not give it.

### Competitor awareness — last, not first

After you have calculated your own price, you may glance at what others seem to charge as a **reality check**. Ask: Are they selling the same scope and standard? Are they counting their labor? Are they sustainable?

If the market sits far from your number, you may adjust **scope**, **positioning**, or **which customers you pursue** — not silently destroy your Margin Guardrail to win every job. Competitor copying is not the BBOS pricing method.

## Handoff to Module 4 — Weekly Operating Rhythm

You now have prices you can defend. That changes how your sales system runs.

In **Module 4 (Weekly Operating Rhythm)**, you will install the weekly cadence that keeps the business moving: plan, do, follow-up, and **the Weekly Close**. Priced proposals belong in that rhythm — presenting a specific Offer Ladder rung at a defined price, moving opportunities to **Proposed**, and running your Follow-Up Cadence so silence does not erase good work.

Module 3 set the numbers. Module 4 makes using them a weekly habit.

Bring these Module 3 outputs into that rhythm:

- Your priced Offer Ladder (so every proposal names a rung and a price).
- Your Price-Hold Script (so weekly selling does not slide into apologizing).
- Your Discount Guardrails and Exception Approval rule (so mid-week pressure does not rewrite your pricing system).

## Forward look to Module 5 — Money and Metrics

Module 3 is **planned** pricing and margin. It does not track whether real jobs matched the Cost Stack, or how cash and runway look over time.

In **Module 5 (Money and Metrics)**, you will review your **Core Number Set** — revenue, margin, cash, and runway — and compare plan to reality. That is where you learn whether your pricing is working in practice. Questions Module 5 will help you answer later:

- Did real labor hours match the Cost Stack assumption?
- Did jobs still clear the Margin Guardrail after overruns?
- Is cash and runway healthy enough for the pace you are selling?

Do not invent a full finance dashboard here. Price with dignity now; measure with clarity later.
